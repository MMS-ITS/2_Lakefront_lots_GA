## Evidence labelling and method

Every finding is tagged:

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[OFFICIAL]** | Government / primary-source data (NOAA, FEMA, USGS, Census, NCES, USACE, GA DCA, GA DPH, county) |
| **[LISTING]** | Commercial listing, directory, or market-aggregator data |
| **[ESTIMATE]** | My own derived figure — arithmetic shown, inputs cited |
| **[UNVERIFIED]** | Found but not confirmable from a primary source, or requires a phone call to confirm |
| **NOT FOUND** | Could not be confirmed; stated as such rather than guessed |

**Two method notes that affect how you should read the numbers:**

1. **Driving distances and times** were computed with the public [OSRM](https://project-osrm.org/)
   routing engine on OpenStreetMap road data. **Distances are reliable. Times are modelled free-flow
   and run conservative (slow)** — particularly on rural two-lane roads. Treat times as upper-bound
   ±25%, and read the *distance* as the harder number. All are tagged [ESTIMATE] for that reason even
   though the underlying road geometry is authoritative.
2. **Point-of-interest discovery** used the [OpenStreetMap Overpass API](https://overpass-api.de/).
   OSM is excellent in metro Atlanta and **sparse in rural Lincoln County** — so for Property B an
   "absence" in OSM is weak evidence of real-world absence, and I say so where it matters.
3. **`api.census.gov` now requires an API key**, which I did not have. All ACS figures below came from
   the [Census Reporter API](https://censusreporter.org/) proxy of the same Census tables. The release
   available is **ACS 2020–2024 5-year**, not 2023 as the brief anticipated — I report what exists.

## 2.6 Karst / sinkhole — not a risk at either site, with direct evidence

**Neither county is karst terrain. Both lots sit on Piedmont crystalline bedrock.** Three independent
lines of evidence:

1. **USGS karst map.** The USGS karst compilation maps karst to *soluble* bedrock — principally
   limestone, dolomite and evaporites. Georgia's karst is confined to the Valley-and-Ridge province of
   the far northwest and the Coastal Plain limestones of the south; the **Piedmont province**, which
   contains both Clayton and Lincoln counties, is non-carbonate crystalline rock and is not mapped as
   karst.
   - [USGS Karst Map of the Conterminous United States — 2020](https://www.usgs.gov/media/images/karst-map-conterminous-united-states-2020)
   - [USGS Karst Aquifers programme page](https://www.usgs.gov/mission-areas/water-resources/science/karst-aquifers)
   - [Weary & Doctor, *Karst in the United States: A Digital Map Compilation and Database*, USGS OFR 2014-1156](https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2014/1156/pdf/of2014-1156.pdf)
   [OFFICIAL]

2. ⭐ **USGS groundwater records name the aquifer directly.** I queried the USGS NWIS site service for
   every groundwater site in both counties
   (`https://waterservices.usgs.gov/nwis/site/?format=rdb&countyCd=13181&siteType=GW&siteOutput=expanded`).
   Every site with an assigned national aquifer code in **both** counties returns
   **`N400PDMBRX` — the Piedmont and Blue Ridge crystalline-rock aquifer.** No carbonate aquifer is
   recorded in either county. This is about as direct as documentary evidence of non-karst geology
   gets. [OFFICIAL]

3. **FEMA NRI** assigns **no karst/sinkhole hazard** to either county, and landslide risk is
   **Very Low** in both (EAL of $218 in Clayton and $44 in Lincoln — effectively zero). [OFFICIAL]

**Consequence for construction:** no sinkhole/subsidence engineering is needed at either site, and
sinkhole insurance is unnecessary. The *opposite* problem applies — crystalline bedrock can be shallow,
which (a) raises excavation and septic-trench cost where rock is encountered, and (b) is why
Georgia DPH denies conventional septic permits where bedrock is less than two feet below the proposed
absorption field (see §11.5). Budget for possible rock; do not budget for sinkholes.

### ⭐ Custom vs production building, and why rural costs MORE

| | Production / spec builder | Custom builder |
|---|---|---|
| Metro Atlanta | ~$136–$170/SF | ~$170–$260/SF (luxury $400+) |
| Augusta / rural east GA | ~$90–$150/SF | ~$150–$220/SF + rural premium |

**Why rural Lincoln County carries a premium despite cheaper land, and metro Atlanta does not:**

- **Mobilization and travel.** There is no meaningful subcontractor base in Lincoln County (population
  7,854). Framers, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, drywall, tile and cabinet crews will travel from
  **Augusta (~50 mi), Thomson (~40 mi), Athens (~64 mi) or Washington (~20 mi)**. Crews price
  windshield time, and they price it *per trip* — so trades that require many short visits (plumbing
  rough/trim, electrical rough/trim, inspections-driven callbacks) are hit hardest.
- **Labour scarcity and scheduling risk.** A single crew's unavailability has no local substitute,
  which lengthens schedules. The **15-month covenant deadline** interacts badly with this: schedule
  pressure forces premium pricing to secure crews.
- **Material delivery.** The nearest big-box supply (Lowe's, Greenwood SC) is **48 miles / ~85
  minutes**; Ace Hardware in Washington GA (19.9 mi / ~44 min) covers consumables but not lumber
  packages or appliances. Delivery surcharges and the cost of a wrong-item return trip are real.
- **Fewer competing bids.** Thin bidder pools mean less price discipline. In metro Atlanta you can get
  five framing bids; in Lincoln County you may get two.
- **Longer utility runs** (see line items) — a rural lot's driveway, water service, power drop and
  propane lines are all multiples of a suburban lot's.

Conversely **metro Atlanta's advantages are real**: deep subcontractor availability, same-day
material access (Home Depot 4.9 mi / ~13 min), competitive bidding, and crews that can absorb a
schedule slip. **Property A's site is harder (wooded, sloped, buffered) but its labour market is far
easier.**

**Rural mobilization premium adopted for Property B: +6% (low) / +9% (likely) / +14% (high) applied
to direct construction costs.** [ESTIMATE — this is my judgement, not a sourced figure]

### ⭐ Georgia septic rules — statutory, and they size the system

**Design flow.** *"Peak flow for residential usage is 150 gallons per day per bedroom."*
— [Georgia DPH](https://dph.georgia.gov/document/document/impact-onsite-sewage-systems-ground-water-nitrogen/download) [OFFICIAL]

> **5 bedrooms × 150 gpd = 750 gallons per day design flow.** The brief's assumption is correct.

**Septic tank capacity — Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. r. 511-3-1-.05(3)**, retrieved verbatim from the
Georgia Secretary of State rules portal (<https://rules.sos.ga.gov/GAC/511-3-1-.05>) [OFFICIAL]:

> *"Capacity. The liquid capacity of septic tanks for single family dwellings shall be one thousand
> gallons for one, two, three or four bedrooms and 250 additional gallons for each bedroom over four.
> Septic tank capacity shall be increased by (50%) if a garbage grinder is to be used."*

| Bedrooms | Required tank (no grinder) | With garbage disposal (+50%) |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | 1,000 gal | 1,500 gal |
| **5** | **1,250 gal** | **1,875 gal** |
| 6 | 1,500 gal | 2,250 gal |

⭐ **Practical tip worth real money:** if you install a garbage disposal in a 5-bedroom house, Georgia
requires a **1,875-gallon** tank instead of 1,250 — a 50% capacity increase. **Omitting the garbage
disposal can save roughly $2,000–4,000** in tank and installation cost. [ESTIMATE]

**Other statutory requirements from the same rule** [OFFICIAL]:
- Two-compartment tank required; first compartment ≥ ⅔ of liquid capacity
- Tanks in series permitted with County Board of Health approval (first tank ≥1,000 gal and ≥⅔ of required capacity)
- **Setbacks:** ≥**50 ft** from wells, springs, sinkholes or suction water lines (and downgrade of
  wells/springs where possible); ≥**25 ft** from lakes, ponds, streams, watercourses and other
  **impoundments**; ≥**10 ft** from pressure water supply lines; ≥**10 ft** from a property line;
  ≥**15 ft** from a drainage ditch or embankment; ideally ≥10 ft from hardscape, drives, pools and
  foundations
- The County Board of Health may allow lesser or require greater distances after site inspection

⚠ **The 25-ft impoundment setback matters at both lots** (reservoir at A, Clarks Hill at B), and at
Property A the **150-ft CCWA reservoir buffer** and 75-ft state-waters buffer are far more
restrictive still — **the septic field and its required reserve area must fit outside all of them.**
That is a genuine site-planning constraint, not a formality. See README §6.3.

**Permit denial conditions** — Georgia DPH denies conventional/chamber septic permits where:
- seasonal high groundwater is **< 2 ft** below the bottom of the absorption field (1 ft with Class I aerobic pretreatment), or
- **bedrock or impervious strata are < 2 ft** below the absorption field bottom (1 ft with Class I pretreatment)

([GA DPH On-Site Sewage Manual, Section B](https://dph.georgia.gov/document/document/envhealthonsitemanual2024-02-22sectionbpdf/download)) [OFFICIAL]

⭐ **This is why §2.6's crystalline-bedrock finding matters commercially.** Both lots sit on Piedmont
crystalline rock. **If rock is shallow at the chosen septic location, a conventional system is
prohibited by rule** and you are pushed to an alternative/pretreatment system — which is where the
$40,000–60,000 high end of my septic line comes from. **Get the soil classification done during due
diligence, before closing, not after.** It costs ~$1,000–2,500 and it is the highest-value
diligence dollar you can spend on either lot.

=> Typical complete well, Lincoln County GA: ~$11,000 - $34,000, likely ~$17,000
```

Compare with the **community water tap at $3,500–$15,000 (likely $7,000)** in the budget. **The
community water system is the cheaper option as well as the covenant-required one** — which is fine,
*provided it actually reaches the lots.*

> ### VERDICT, PROPERTY A: **VALUE-DESTRUCTIVE.**
> At the likely cost, you would spend **~$834,000 to create an asset the local market currently
> values at $565,000 at the very top of its range** — a paper loss of roughly **$269,000 before you
> have paid a dollar for the land.** Add the lot and the loss widens further. **Even the optimistic
> low-cost scenario does not clear the top comp.**

To break even against the $565,000 top comp you would need an all-in build cost of
**$565,000 ÷ 3,500 SF = $161/SF**, which is *below* the NAHB national average direct cost of
$161.78/SF **before any builder profit, contingency, septic, driveway or tree clearing**. That is not
achievable for custom construction. [ESTIMATE]

**Mitigating factors that genuinely soften this** — and they are real:
- The tract's demographics support higher-end housing far better than county data suggests:
  **median household income $119,872, 90.8% owner-occupancy, 88.8% family households** (§3.2).
  A $700k+ home is not absurd *for this tract* even if there is no comp for it yet.
- Lakefront (even reservoir-front) lots in a recorded, established subdivision are scarce.
- The brief's comp range tops out at $565k, but those are *existing* homes of unknown age, size and
  condition. A new 3,500 SF 5-bedroom custom home is a different product and could plausibly set a
  new high-water mark — **but you would be the price discoverer, which is an expensive role.**
- **No apparent Clayton impact fee** (§11.5) saves perhaps $6,000 vs the national norm.

**Value-engineering path to reduce the loss:** build **3,200 SF** (the bottom of the brief's range)
with production-grade rather than custom finishes, confirm sewer availability to delete the $26,000
septic, and target **$190–200/SF all-in ≈ $608,000–640,000**. Still above the top comp, but the gap
narrows from ~$269k to ~$50–75k — which is a defensible premium for a brand-new custom home in a
high-income tract. **This is the realistic plan if you build here.** [ESTIMATE]

To match the $422,000 comp at 3,100 SF you would need **$136/SF all-in**, which is **below the
national average direct construction cost** and far below what rural mobilization permits. Not
achievable. [ESTIMATE]

**And value-engineering does not rescue it.** A deliberately modest 2,400 SF production-grade build:

```

+ rural mobilization premium @ 9%      =  $37,800
                                         --------

**The structural problem is not the design — it is the market.** Lincoln County's tract median home
value of **$152,600**, median household income of **$42,912**, and 25.8%-retiree age profile simply
do not support $900k custom homes. There is no local buyer pool. Resale would depend entirely on
out-of-area lake buyers, which is a thin and cyclical market.

| | **Property A** | **Property B** |
|---|---|---|

> ### The honest conclusion
>
> **Building a 5-bedroom custom home is value-destructive on BOTH lots, and roughly twice as bad on
> Property B.** Neither should be undertaken as an investment or on the assumption of recovering cost
> at resale. Both only make sense if the buyer:
> 1. intends to **hold long-term and live there**, treating the premium as consumption rather than
>    investment; and
> 2. **can absorb a $270k (A) or $520k (B) paper loss** without distress; and
> 3. values the specific amenity — metro access and schools at A, lake life and quiet at B — enough
>    to pay for it.
>
> **If the goal is a large home at or near its cost, the right move on either lot is to buy an
> existing home rather than build.** In Waterpointe, $541k–$565k already buys a finished large home
> — against $834k to build one. That is a ~$280,000 saving for substantially the same product.
>
> **If you build anyway, Property A is the materially better bet:** lower $/SF, a tract whose
> $119,872 median income and $329,400 median home value can plausibly support a premium home, deeper
> contractor competition, and no dock-permit uncertainty. It also carries one discrete gating risk to
> clear first — the **Clayton County residential moratorium** (§11.5).
